# Write an X Article

## Use when

- The owner wants a long-form piece on X: an essay, a how-to or protocol, an operator playbook, a curated list (for example a weekly events edit), an analysis of news, an investigation, or a founder story.
- A short post or a thread is a different skill. Turning a newsletter, LinkedIn post, or doc into an Article is this skill: rewrite it for X, do not paste it.
- The output is a draft. Never publish, schedule, approve, or turn on auto-publish. The owner reviews every draft, and a yes covers only that exact text, media, and timing.

## What X gives you

Check these in the editor. X's own help page could not be fetched when this skill was written, so the specs come from secondary sources.

- Title of about 100 characters, an optional cover image, and a body with headings, subheadings, bold, italic, strikethrough, numbered and bulleted lists, block quotes, links, inline images, GIFs, and embedded posts.
- Cover: 5:2 ratio, designed at 2000x800 px. The reader view can crop to a wider strip, so keep text and faces in a centered band about 60% of the height. JPG or PNG, sRGB, under 5 MB.
- The feed card shows the cover, the title, and the opening lines. Those three are the whole ad. Most readers decide there.
- Publishing needs X Premium. Reading is free.

## What separates 15M+ view articles from the rest

From a study of 7 articles above 15M views and about 20 between 1M and 15M (see `references/research.md`). These are patterns, not guarantees. Reach also depends on the author's audience and timing.

1. **Stakes are universal, not niche.** Every 15M+ piece is about something a stranger cares about: their whole life, their job, their money, their country, their kids' school. Pieces in the 1M to 15M tier tend to serve one audience well (traders, founders, a storm region). Niche can still win if the frame is "what this means for you".
2. **The emotional promise is total.** "Fix your entire life", "something bigger than Covid", "$74 billion", "the exact playbook". The title promises a complete answer or a large hidden truth, not a tip.
3. **Timing rides a moment everyone is already feeling.** New Year resolutions, a model release week, a tariff announcement that morning, a culture-war fight that week. The article lands while the feed is primed.
4. **Earned authority in the first 150 words.** Proof of work, not credentials: "I sorted through millions of invoices", "12 months analyzing every tariff move", "six years building in this space", "I almost could not believe what I was hearing." The reader learns why this person can say it before the first header.
5. **The opening names the reader's private feeling.** The top pieces say something the reader felt but had not put into words (resolutions feel fake, multiple interests feel like a weakness, AI news feels overblown). Mid-tier pieces more often open with information.
6. **A complete system, not a list of tips.** The top how-tos end with a protocol the reader can run today. The top analysis gives numbered steps for the next 2 to 4 weeks. The top investigation gives the dataset.
7. **Length follows depth, and the long ones are very long.** 15M+ pieces ran 1,000 to 7,200 words. The essays and how-tos sat at 4,800 to 5,500. The short winners (about 1,000 to 1,200 words) are dense with hard facts and dated steps.
8. **Shareability is engineered.** Bookmarks often exceed likes (one piece has about 1.0M bookmarks to 381K likes). The articles ask for it in the intro ("bookmark this, you will want it in two weeks") or the close ("send this to someone who needs it").
9. **Structure is visible from the headers alone.** Roman numerals or "Step #1 to #10", headers written as claims, and one objection turned into a header. A skimmer gets the argument without reading the body.
10. **One clear close.** A recap of the system, then one CTA (share, bookmark, subscribe, follow), often with a line that sets up the next piece.

Mid-tier gaps to avoid: a title that only describes the topic, an opening that starts with background, no proof of work, tips with no system, and two or three competing CTAs.

## Steps

1. **Collect the source.** Owner notes, data, events, past posts. List every fact, number, name, and quote you may use and where it came from. Missing facts stay as `[TK]`. Never fill a gap.
2. **Pick the type** from the templates below.
3. **Write the brief in 6 lines.** The reader. The private feeling or problem they have. The one promise. The proof of work the author really has. The moment that makes it timely now. What the reader does after.
4. **Title.** Write 8 options using the formulas. Score them with the title test. Keep the best one and 2 alternates for the owner.
5. **Hook (first 150 to 300 words).** Pick a pattern. Then run the opening sequence below.
6. **Outline.** Write the headers first. Read them alone. If they do not tell the whole argument, fix them before writing the body.
7. **Draft.** Paragraphs of 1 to 3 sentences, many one-line paragraphs, one idea per section, concrete before abstract. Use the owner's own examples and words.
8. **Format.** A header every 150 to 400 words. Numbered steps for sequences. Bold only the line a skimmer must not miss. A block quote or a one-line paragraph for the key idea. An image only where it proves or explains a claim.
9. **Close.** Recap the system or the main idea in 2 to 4 lines, give one CTA, and add an optional next-piece line.
10. **Cover brief** for the designer, with 2 or 3 directions.
11. **Teaser post** for the feed, plus one follow-up post idea.
12. **Score it with the rubric.** Fix anything under the bar. Hand back the draft, the score, the title alternates, and open questions.

## Titles

### Formulas (in our own words, modeled on top performers)

- **Total outcome, short time:** How to [change something big] in [surprisingly short time].
- **Promise plus a twist:** How to [outcome] ([the counterintuitive method]).
- **Blunt command or warning:** If you [are X], do not [waste Y]. / You need to [uncomfortable thing] to [outcome].
- **Ominous understatement:** [Something] is happening. / [Thing] is under attack.
- **Exposé with a number:** [Named institution], a $[size] [strong metaphor].
- **The exact playbook:** [Actor]'s EXACT [topic] playbook, a step-by-step guide.
- **Myth flip:** [Common belief] is wrong. [What actually happened].
- **Size of the prize:** $[size] opportunity: [trend].
- **Named framework:** [Framework name]: a map to [outcome].
- **How I work:** [Tool or method] for [job]. / How I use [tool] to [outcome].
- **Series:** [Brand]'s edit: week of [date] in [city].

### Title test (each yes is a point; aim for 4 or more)

- Would a stranger outside the niche care?
- Does it promise a complete answer or a hidden truth, not a tip?
- Does it hold a concrete noun or a number from the source?
- Is there tension (a twist, a warning, a contradiction)?
- Is it under about 70 characters, so the card shows all of it?
- Can it be said out loud in one breath?

Avoid: a topic label ("Thoughts on AI"), a question with an obvious answer, and stacked colons or brackets.

## Hooks

### Opening sequence (first 150 to 300 words)

1. **Line 1 grabs.** A contrarian line, a shared memory, a confession, a scene, or a hard fact.
2. **Name the private feeling.** "You have felt X. You were told Y."
3. **Proof of work.** Why the author can say this, in one or two lines.
4. **The promise.** What the reader will have by the end, with the count of ideas or steps if there is one.
5. **Optional save or share line.** "Bookmark this, you will want it when..." or "send this to someone who..."
6. **A bridge line.** "Let's begin." / "Here is what we found."

### Patterns with annotated examples (written for this skill, not copied)

- **Contrarian opener.** "Most advice about cold email is written by people who have never had to hit a quota." *Picks a fight with the default and signals an insider view.*
- **Shared memory.** "Remember the week everyone suddenly had a podcast?" *Borrows a feeling the reader already has, then points it at the new topic.*
- **Self-diagnosis.** "Tell me if this sounds like you: you have 40 tabs open, 3 half-built projects, and a calendar full of other people's priorities." *The reader recognizes themself and keeps reading to see the fix.*
- **Scene cold open.** "At 11:40 p.m., the dashboard showed zero replies for the third week in a row." *Puts the reader in a moment before the argument starts.*
- **Hard fact plus stakes.** "This morning, [event]. Here is what it means for the next two weeks." *Timely and useful. Works for analysis.*
- **Aphorism.** Two mirrored sentences that flip the topic. *Quotable, so it gets screenshotted.*
- **Plain promise.** "This is exactly how our team runs [process], step by step." *Strong for operator playbooks. Credibility comes from specificity.*
- **Reader question.** "The question we get most this month: why is X happening if Y?" *Starts from real demand.*

## Templates by type

**Essay or point of view** (2,500 to 5,500 words)
Hook, then stakes for the reader, then "I know because..." (first-hand), then 5 to 7 claim headers with one objection as a header, then what to actually do, then the bigger picture, then a short punchy close with a share line.

**How-to or protocol** (2,500 to 5,500 words)
Hook, then why most people fail, then the reframe, then numbered sections (I to VII, or Step 1 to N), each with what, how, and an example, then a protocol the reader can run today (time blocks, exact prompts or questions), then the system recap, then a sign-off.

**Operator playbook, how I work** (900 to 3,000 words)
"This is how I run X", then why I am sharing it and what changed, then setup tips, then one header per component, role, or stage (2 to 4 sentences plus a screenshot each), then the before and after in plain terms (only real figures), then a soft CTA (try it, reply with what you would add).

**Analysis or news explainer** (1,000 to 3,000 words)
News peg or reader question, then the thesis in one line, then the pattern as "Step #1 to #N" or claim headers, each followed by a chart or source, then "what happens next" with a time window, then a conclusion and a follow line.

**Investigation or exposé** (1,000 to 5,000 words)
"You were not meant to see this" frame, then proof of work, then the numbers, then a fast list of documented cases, then who benefits, then the data or sources with links. Every claim needs a source. Never publish an accusation the owner cannot back.

**Curated list or weekly edit** (200 to 1,200 words)
Series title with the date, then a one-line intro (how many items were screened, only if counted), then the top pick first, then each item as a "Day, date: Name" header with a one-line "why", time, place, host, and the RSVP link, then an optional "if you only go to one" or "off the laptop" section, then when the next edition drops and a public "tag someone" CTA. Keep the same format every week so readers learn it.

**Founder story or lesson** (700 to 2,000 words)
Cold open on a dated moment, then what happened in plain beats, then the turn ("here is what it taught me"), then 2 or 3 lessons, then how I operate now, then a warm close. Few or no headers.

## Images and cover

- Inline images: charts for analysis (one per claim), screenshots for playbooks, diagrams for frameworks, none for pure essays. Each image needs a reason in the outline.
- Cover brief template:
  - Size 2000x800 (5:2). Safe band is centered, about 60% of the height. Nothing important near the edges.
  - Either 2 to 5 large words from the title in high contrast, or one striking image with no text. Readable at phone feed size.
  - Brand colors and fonts from the brand files. One focal element. A fixed look for any series.
  - Give 2 or 3 directions, for example bold type on a brand color, an illustrated scene, or a real photo with an overlay. Never a generated face or body of the owner.

## Promotion (drafts only)

- **Teaser post:** either the article link alone (works for large accounts) or 1 to 2 lines that state the tension or the promise, then the link. Smaller accounts should add the lines.
- **Follow-up post idea:** the single most quotable line or the best list item, for later the same day or the next day.
- **Series line:** end with what comes next ("next week: ...") when the piece is part of a series.
- Never post, schedule, like, quote, reply, or DM. Hand the drafts to the owner.

## Scoring rubric (grade before handoff)

Score each 0 to 2. Hand off at 16 or more out of 22, with no zero on rows 1, 6, or 10.

| # | Check | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Source integrity | Any invented number, quote, or story | Some claims unsourced but marked `[TK]` | Every fact traces to the source list |
| 2 | Title | Topic label | Clear but flat | Passes 4+ title-test checks |
| 3 | Line 1 | Background or throat clearing | Decent | Stops the scroll on its own |
| 4 | Private feeling | Missing | Implied | Named plainly in the first 150 words |
| 5 | Proof of work | Missing | Credentials only | Specific effort or experience in the first 150 words |
| 6 | Promise kept | Body does not deliver the title | Partly | Fully, with a system or clear takeaway |
| 7 | Headers | Missing or vague | Some claims | Headers alone tell the argument |
| 8 | Skimmability | Walls of text | Mixed | 1 to 3 sentence paragraphs, steps numbered, key lines alone |
| 9 | Images | Decorative or missing where needed | Some help | Each one proves or explains a claim |
| 10 | House rules | Any break | n/a | All pass |
| 11 | Close and CTA | None or several | One but weak | Recap plus one public CTA |

## Checklist

- [ ] Type picked and 6-line brief written.
- [ ] Title under 100 characters (ideally under about 70), with 2 alternates.
- [ ] The opening sequence is done within 300 words.
- [ ] Every number and name traces to the source list. Unknowns are `[TK]`.
- [ ] The headers alone tell the argument.
- [ ] Images each have a reason.
- [ ] One CTA, public.
- [ ] No em or en dashes, no hashtags, at most one emoji.
- [ ] Links are full and correct, with no shorteners.
- [ ] Cover brief, teaser post, and follow-up idea included.
- [ ] Rubric score written on the handoff.
- [ ] Nothing published or scheduled.

## House rules

- No invented numbers, quotes, anecdotes, customers, or results. Use only what the source supplies. Unknown is not zero.
- No em dashes or en dashes anywhere. Use commas, periods, or colons.
- No hashtags. At most one emoji in the whole article.
- Links: inline links are fine inside an Article because it is one native document. Keep source and RSVP links next to their item, put product or signup links near the close, and keep the teaser post to the article link plus optional lines.
- CTAs are public: share, bookmark, reply with thoughts, tag a friend, try the product, subscribe. Never "DM me", "I will send you", or "comment KEYWORD".
- Do not name competitors or other creators unless the owner asks. Reference articles are craft only. Never copy their lines, stories, structure verbatim, or numbers.
- Drafting only. Never publish, schedule, approve, or enable auto-publish. If the text, media, audience, or timing changes after a yes, ask again.
